  • No study tests this exact view, so the answer rests on nearby evidence: about 200 sources across research papers, practitioners' writing and products.

What works, need by need

Three needs, three forms

1

See the big picture of what you think

A few topics, each with two to four of your ideas in your own words, each with one piece of your work. Conflicts shown side by side; "open" and "changed since you last looked" marked; the rest one step away.
Research, practitioners' hub pages and products all point here. Graphs are backed only for seeing shape and following links, at small size.
weak to moderate
2

Get back into a piece of work

Where you left off: the work as you left it (its last state and last few steps), what changed since, and your stated goal and next step. One click opens the work itself.
Studies of resuming work back the work as you left it as the best cue. A summary is only a label.
moderate
3

Answer a question about your past

Ask in the chat. You get exact quotes from your own chats first, each showing where it came from and opening that place. If nothing is found, the answer says where it looked.
The strongest agreement across products. It is also how you used the "Ask about this" button.
moderate, strongest agreement

What changes from sketch B

  • Far fewer entries per topic
  • Your own words, not the AI's rewording
  • A piece of your work on every entry

What stays, as you asked

  • Connect and conflict lines

The evidence that decides the details

What the studies show

  • People recalled an email's topic far more often than when it was sent. So topics come first and time is only a filter.85.1% vs 57.5%Elsweiler, Baillie and Ruthven, ACM TOIS 2008, email re-finding
  • A map beat an outline only slightly for students, and made no real difference for strong learners. You're the expert on your own ideas, so your preference can pick the shape.g = 0.28Nesbit and Adesope 2006, meta-analysis of 10 studies comparing maps with outlines or lists
  • ChatGPT's memory answered far fewer questions about past chats than the same model reading the full history. So answers come from your saved chats, quoted.57.7% vs 91.8%LongMemEval pilot, ICLR 2025
  • One wrong detail in an AI summary changed what people remembered seeing. So your ideas appear in your own words.83.6% → 44.8%Sim, Eiger and Kohno, AIES 2026, 328 people
  • People who learned from an AI's synthesis knew the topic less deeply than people who searched for themselves.n = 10,462Melumad and Yun, PNAS Nexus 2025, 7 experiments
  • Citations raised trust even when they were random. So every source must open the real place.n ≈ 300Ding et al., AAAI 2025
  • Asking can lose to plain search. Google paused Ask Photos for being slow, then brought it back with ordinary results shown first.June 2025Google Photos product lead; press coverage
  • No maker of a graph view publishes how often it's used. In 2025 they added tables and chat instead. NotebookLM's mind map turns a click into a question in the chat.no dataObsidian, Heptabase and Google help pages; research note 26d
Looking back

Why the sketches fell short

  • Sketch A listed everything, like a full table of contents, in the AI's words. Practitioners' hub pages list only a few entry points.
  • Sketch B showed the shape but not the meaning. It drew every line at once, and its entries led nowhere new.
  • Both covered only the big picture, while your questions were about your past. And the timed lookups felt like a quiz, because asking does that job best.
