# Round 6 research, 26c: what practitioners have published about revisiting your own ideas

**Date:** 2026-10-27. One of four parallel notes on the overview's form. This one covers practitioners' and thinkers' published writing; the others cover re-finding and resumption research, visualization and learning science, and products.

**Short answer.**
- For the big picture, practitioners converge on one form: a small, curated **entry layer**. Luhmann's keyword index, Ahrens's entry points, structure notes and Milo's "maps of content" are all short lists of starting points, most with a line of context. Their "maps" are text, not graphs [E].
- They also agree that structure grows from use and gets rebuilt, that revisiting follows current work, that the concrete thing beats its label, and that history is added to, not overwritten [E].
- Graph views are defended only for upkeep (spotting orphans, seeing the shape of a collection), never for navigating or understanding [E].
- To get back into work, they leave a note for the next session (the "Hemingway bridge") and keep a project's material together. Lab data back writing the next step down [E].
- To answer questions about their own past, the tool-builders (Bush, Luhmann, Johnson, Lee) ask, then follow a trail of associations. They do not browse a map [E].
- Almost none of this rests on data. Measured results exist only for memory prompts (Quantum Country), for how rarely knowledge workers consult their files (Kidd, 12 people), and for plan-making in the lab. Hubs, maps of content, graphs, reviews and resurfacing rest on experience and argument [E].
- The main tension: practitioners say that making the overview is where the understanding happens, and Obsidian's CEO declines to hand his upkeep to a language model. Northstar's overview is AI-built, and this user keeps few notes. Practitioners who do use machines let them propose links, and the person adopts them [E for the positions, L for the application].
- So: lead with a short hub of the person's own ideas, each with a work cue. Make "ask", with the answer returned as a trail, the main path for needs 2 and 3. Offer the map as a second view of shape and conflicts [L].

**Question:** which forms do experienced practitioners and thinkers recommend for revisiting and re-understanding one's own ideas, why, and on what evidence? What does that say about the overview's three needs: (1) see the big picture of what I think and know; (2) get back into a piece of work; (3) answer a question about my own past?

**Method:** one desk pass on 2026-10-27, about 90 tool calls, with primary sources opened where possible.
- **[E]** = a source shows it: opened today, quoted from the page in a search result, or in the earlier note named. **[L]** = my inference. **Unverified** = seen only in a secondary source or a search summary.
- Recommendations are graded **[data]** (measured outcomes), **[experience]** (the author's or users' reported practice) or **[argument]** (reasoning, analogy or theory).
- Sources are paraphrased. Titles and terms of art are given as names; the only words quoted directly are the user's own.
- No personal data was read. Findings constrain the HOW only (GOAL §5).

## 1. Foundations: trails and views

### Vannevar Bush, "As We May Think" (1945)
- **Diagnosis.** Bush blamed slow access to the record on artificial indexing. An item sits in one place, you find it by working down classes, and each new path means starting again. The mind, he said, works by association [E].
- **The form: a trail.** The memex user joins two items with a tap, adds comments, names the trail and enters the name in a code book. Years later, a new question brings the trail back [E].
- **His example.** A reader builds a trail on why the short Turkish bow beat the English longbow, with side trails into elasticity and tables of physical constants. Later, in a talk about why people resist new ideas, he recalls the trail and uses it [E].
- **Sharing.** A trail can be copied to a friend and joined to the friend's own. Bush imagined a new profession of "trail blazers" [E].
- **Memory.** He accepts that trails not followed often fade in the mind; the machine's record would not [E].
- **Evidence.** Argument and extrapolation from 1940s devices. He calls the essay a suggestion, not a prophecy [E]. **Grade: [argument].**
- **For Northstar** [L]. A trail is a path through your own material, with your own notes on it, recalled when a new question arrives. It serves needs 2 and 3. Bush never proposed a map of everything as the way back in.

### Douglas Engelbart, "Augmenting Human Intellect" (1962)
- **One structure, many views.** Engelbart separated what is stored from what is shown. The computer reshapes the stored structure into a view that fits the moment; he called this view generation [E].
- **Joe's views.** In the report's imagined demonstration, a user named Joe links statements with typed, directed links. He asks for a statement's direct antecedents, then reverses the arrows to see its consequents. He asks for a network view with antecedents above, magnified where he is looking [E].
- **Revisit.** Joe says that when he sets a problem aside, the structure is left well organized for a later return [E].
- **The built system.** NLS could show a file's outline down to the second level, three lines per statement, and change the view with other commands (Müller-Prove's history). The name "view specs" was not confirmed in the source opened [E].
- **Evidence.** A hypothetical user and argument. The few reported outcomes do not cover re-entering one's own work [E]. **Grade: [argument].**
- **For Northstar** [L]. Outline and map are views of one structure, not rival products. The antecedent view answers "why do I think this?", a need-3 question that neither sketch offered.

## 2. The slip-box tradition: index, entry points and structure notes

### Niklas Luhmann, "Communicating with Slip Boxes" (1981)
- **No topical order.** A fixed topic scheme would commit him to one order for decades. Instead each card gets a fixed number, and new cards branch in anywhere [E].
- **Entry by register.** Nobody remembers numbers, so he kept a keyword register and kept updating it. From an entry he followed links [E].
- **What the archive shows** (note 02). The register listed only one to four cards per keyword. He rebuilt it four times. Poorly linked regions went dead [E].
- **A partner, not a store.** The box answers a query with combinations he never planned. Finding things depends partly on chance. The whole is a disorder with an inner structure [E].
- **Evidence.** More than 26 years of use, framed in systems theory. He says he cannot settle the design question by deduction [E]. **Grade: [experience] + [argument].**

### Sönke Ahrens, *How to Take Smart Notes* (2017)
Read through Matuschak's reading notes; the book was not opened [E, secondary].
- Keywords are few and chosen with care. Every note can be reached from the index or from an entry-point note; links do the rest [E].
- He swaps the archivist's question (which topic does this belong under?) for the writer's: in what context will I want to come across this again? [E].
- Revisiting notes in changing contexts approximates spaced repetition. Writing a task down lets you resume it later without holding it in mind; he invokes the Zeigarnik effect [E].
- **Evidence.** Luhmann's output, plus learning-science findings named in general terms [E]. **Grade: [experience], with borrowed [data] from lab studies of learning, not of revisiting.**

### Structure notes (Sascha Fast, zettelkasten.de, 2018)
- A structure note is a table of contents for a set of notes. It adds the order and hierarchy a tag lacks [E].
- By his recollection, his first hub-like notes appeared at about 500–700 notes. Three layers then emerged without a plan: content notes, structure notes and top-level structure notes. Most grew bottom-up; a few were planned [E].
- He compares the archive to a deep sea where you see a few inches at a time. A structure note shows how one region is laid out [E].
- **Evidence.** His own archive; no data [E]. **Grade: [experience].**

### Scott Scheper, the Antinet (2022)
- Four principles: analog cards, numeric-alpha addresses, a branching tree, and an index. You enter through the index [E].
- He criticizes fluid digital systems built on links and tags, and rigid library schemes alike [E].
- **Evidence.** His own box, analogy, and some weak citations; a machine-learning paper is used to support a claim about memory [E]. **Grade: [experience] + [argument].**

**What the tradition agrees on** [E]. You enter through a few curated starting points, not a full table of contents. Starting points are added when a region gets crowded. After entry, most movement follows links. Hierarchy is disputed: Luhmann avoided topical order but kept top-level sections, Fast adds hierarchy in structure notes, and Scheper insists on a tree.

## 3. Andy Matuschak and Michael Nielsen

- **Association over hierarchy.** Matuschak's evergreen notes are concept-oriented and densely linked. He argues against filing them in a hierarchy, because early sorting fixes categories before the real ones are visible [E]. **Grade: [argument]** (he cites Bush and Ahrens).
- **The weakness he names.** In a linked system it is hard to reach a note's "neighbors": notes that are related but not linked to it. A hub note helps only partly, because you must leave for the hub and lose the backlinks in view. His suggested remedy is outline notes with an authored order, plus an interface that moves between neighbors through them. He judges tags ineffective [E]. **Grade: [argument].**
- **Even he gives a start page.** His public notes have no index or navigation aids, and he points new readers to a "top of mind" note to begin [E].
- **Revisiting by working.** Keeping a linked collection current makes him reread old notes, so upkeep works like spaced repetition. But the schedule follows his present interests, neglected topics decay, and it is weak for memory because there is no self-testing [E]. **Grade: [experience].**
- **Spaced prompts for unripe ideas.** He proposes showing a few "inklings" from a writing inbox each morning: fruitful ones return soon, unfruitful ones much later. It is a proposal from his practice; he reports no results [E]. **Grade: [experience].**
- **Peripheral vision.** Physical cues prompt action without a scheduled review, keep things concrete and keep their context. Digital highlight lists strip that context away [E].
- **But unchanging cues fade.** A cue that never changes stops being seen; a pile on his desk dropped out of notice within a month [E]. **Grade: [experience].**
- **"Why books don't work" (2019).** Understanding needs active engagement; reading alone transmits less than readers think. He says plainly that his claim about how little readers absorb rests on intuition, not studies [E]. **Grade: [argument].**
- **Quantum Country's data** (with Nielsen, "How can we develop transformative tools for thought?", 2019). The only measured numbers in this slice [E]:
  - 112 questions sit inside an essay that takes about four hours to read;
  - about 95 minutes of total review, to reach six repetitions, gave about 54 days of demonstrated retention per question on average;
  - six months after launch, 195 users had shown a month of retention on at least 80% of their cards;
  - in a two-week experiment with groups of 16 and 25 users, readers who kept reviewing went from 89% to 96% accuracy, while readers whose review was delayed fell from 91% to 87%.

  The authors call the results preliminary, and the readers are self-selected. **Grade: [data], for remembering an essay's content through designed prompts. It does not test revisiting one's own ideas through an overview.**

## 4. Second-brain practice: Forte and Milo

### Tiago Forte
- **PARA.** Organize by what you are acting on (projects, areas, resources, archives), not by school subjects. A busy person has no time to dig through a broad category for one fact. Finished projects move to the archive [E]. **Grade: [experience]**: a decade of his own practice and teaching thousands, with no outcome data [E].
- **Progressive summarization.** Design each note for a future self who decides in seconds whether it is worth reading. Add layers (bold, highlight, a short summary in your own words) only when you revisit the note for real work. He accepts that every compression loses context [E]. **Grade: [experience].**
- **Intermediate packets.** Keep reusable pieces, such as a slide, a paragraph or a diagram, so new work can be assembled from parts [E]. **Grade: [argument]** (analogies to modules, sketches and scrimmages).
- **Weekly and monthly reviews.** Taken from David Allen's *Getting Things Done*. Forte urges a short review, sometimes split over several days, and counts reviews among the habits that matter most (secondary sources; unverified) [E]. **Grade: [experience].**
- **The Hemingway bridge.** Chapter 8 of *Building a Second Brain* names a habit drawn from Hemingway's 1958 *Paris Review* interview: end a session knowing what comes next, and write it down for next time. The book's endnotes confirm the chapter; the technique's detail comes from a secondary summary (unverified) [E]. **Grade: [experience].**

### Nick Milo, maps of content (MOCs)
- A MOC is a note that gathers links to related notes and arranges them in context. You make one when scattered notes start to overwhelm you, a moment he calls the "mental squeeze point" [E].
- MOCs sit on top of notes without moving them, and several can map the same notes differently. Their jobs are to gather, develop and navigate ideas [E].
- **Evidence.** His experience and worked examples, with a loose appeal to the memory palace; no data [E]. **Grade: [experience].**
- **Worth noticing** [E]. A MOC is a page of headed, annotated links: an outline. "Map" names its role, not a picture.

## 5. Gardens, files, links and search

- **Maggie Appleton, digital gardens.** Gardens are arranged by context and association rather than by date; she calls this "topography over timelines". They offer many entry points and no set path, and each note shows its growth stage. Feeds, she argues, are not built to accumulate understanding [E]. **Grade: [argument]**, with examples.
- **Appleton on language models** ("Squish Meets Structure", 2023). Models should be small reasoning steps, not sources of truth. A bare chat box leaves most of the work to the user. Small, specific tools beat one "magic" tool [E]. **Grade: [experience]** (as a designer at Elicit) + [argument].
- **Steph Ango (Obsidian's CEO), "How I use Obsidian".** Few folders; links and a categories property. He moves by quick switcher, backlinks and links, and uses a local graph at shallow depth to see related notes. He does not mention the global graph [E].
- **Ango's reviews.** Every few months he does a random revisit. He compiles captured fragments every few days, then reviews those monthly and the monthly reviews yearly ("fractal journaling") [E].
- **Ango on automation.** Asked whether a language model could do this upkeep, he declines: he enjoys it, and doing it is how he understands his own patterns. He links his essay "Don't delegate understanding", which argues the general case and does not mention AI. "File over app" adds that durable plain files let you return decades later [E]. **Grade: [experience] + [argument].**
- **Gordon Brander, "All you need is links" (2022).** Links are the one primitive. A tag is a set of backlinks, a folder is a page of links, and an outline is one view over linked pages. Counting links shows the most-discussed topics [E]. **Grade: [argument].** No essay by Brander on revisiting was found.
- **Linus Lee, "Incremental note-taking" (2021).** Ideas that cannot be recalled are worthless. Time should be a first-class axis. Add new entries rather than overwrite, so past states stay visible [E].
- **Lee's Monocle (2021).** He built a search engine over his own writing, journals and notes, to get a better memory without remembering more. In its first days he searched it as often as Google [E].
- **Lee's "Hyperlink maximalism".** Machines should generate links on demand, alongside hand-made ones. Machine-made ideas should appear only when the reader clicks [E]. **Grade: [experience]** (his own prototypes; no data).
- **Steven Johnson, "Tool for Thought" (*New York Times*, 2005).** He kept his own writing and a few thousand chosen quotes in DEVONthink. He pasted in a paragraph and followed the similar passages it returned, building a trail of associations. The biggest gain was finding things he had forgotten entirely. Quoted via a 2005 blog; the original page was blocked [E]. **Grade: [experience].**

## 6. Tools-for-thought research: Ink & Switch and Bret Victor

- **Ink & Switch, Capstone.** Interviews with dozens of creative professionals found the same two steps: collect raw material, then think by sorting, rearranging and looking from new angles, on walls of cards, pinboards and mood boards. One of their design principles: show the thing itself (an image), not its abstraction (a filename) [E]. **Grade: [experience]**: qualitative interviews and prototype tests, with no counts.
- **Bret Victor, "Magic Ink" (2006).** Most software is used to learn, so its design is mostly graphic design. Show enough to compare and decide. Treat interaction as a last resort. Infer context from the situation and from history instead of making the user ask [E]. **Grade: [argument]**, with redesign examples.
- **For Northstar** [L]. An overview opened for a purpose should use that purpose, the chat it was opened from, as context. It should show the work itself, and enough at once to decide. Capstone's "the thing, not the filename" matches the user's own finding that a path string does not help (GOAL §2).

## 7. Critiques

### Graph views
- **Critics.** Practitioner write-ups call Obsidian's global graph attractive but mostly decorative at scale, with every link drawn alike. These are blog posts of low authority [E].
- **The Zettelkasten forum.** One user finds graph views useful for young collections and useless once the network is dense. Others rely on structure notes and incoming-link counts instead (via search summaries) [E].
- **A defender.** Eleanor Konik uses the global graph to check her system: to spot orphans and sync errors, and to judge how developed an idea's region is. Asked whether she uses it to navigate, she says she does not [E].
- **Studies.** In the one interview study of Obsidian users, the graph view was not among the retrieval strategies (notes 02 and 25) [E]. No study was found that measures whether a graph helps people understand their own ideas.
- **Grade.** [experience], for upkeep only.

### Collecting without using
- **Alison Kidd, "The Marks are on the Knowledge Worker" (CHI 1994).** Twelve knowledge workers rarely consulted their filed information and carried little with them. She concluded that the value lies in having been informed, so tools should support informing rather than filing [E, from the abstract and search excerpts]. **Grade: [data]**, small and qualitative.
- **Casey Newton** (*Platformer*, about 2022). He built daily notes and links, waited for insights that never came, and concluded that the tools store but do not help him think. Seen only in secondary summaries; wording and date unverified [E].
- **Anne-Laure Le Cunff, Ness Labs.** Move from note-taking to note-making: rephrase, relate, revisit. She cites the generation effect and some studies she does not name (unverified) [E]. **Grade: [argument].**
- **Sellen & Whittaker, "Beyond Total Capture" (CACM 2010).** Rather than capture everything, design for how human memory works. Secondary coverage lists five uses (recollecting, reminiscing, retrieving, reflecting, remembering intentions) and reports their view that little evidence shows lifelogs help with any of them (unverified) [E].
- Westenberg, Tietze and the write-only data are in note 02.

### AI doing the organizing
- Ango declines (§5). On the Zettelkasten forum, users keep the main decisions themselves, and a reply attributed to Fast calls an AI-generated "key insight" empty (via search summary; unverified) [E].
- Appleton and Lee accept machine help as small, inspectable steps or as links shown on demand; Johnson's machine associations ran over an archive he had chosen [E]. Note 02 adds Karpathy's LLM-maintained wiki and Arango's "amanuensis" view.

## 8. Resurfacing practices

| Practice | Who | What it does | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily notes, time-first logs | Lee; Ango | Lets you rewind to a past state of mind | [experience] |
| Weekly and monthly reviews | Allen, via Forte | Re-decide what matters; short and regular | [experience]. Heylighen & Vidal (2008) argue that GTD fits situated and distributed cognition [argument] |
| Writing the next step | Hemingway, via Forte; Ahrens | Frees the mind and eases the restart | Masicampo & Baumeister (2011): specific plans for unfinished goals removed intrusive thoughts and interference in lab tasks [data, indirect] |
| Reviews of reviews | Ango | Days, then a month, then a year | [experience] |
| Random revisit | Ango | Rediscover old notes, add links | [experience] |
| Upkeep driven by interest | Matuschak; Ahrens | Revisiting follows current work | [experience] |
| Scheduled highlights | Readwise | A daily review; "keep" brings a highlight back in about a week; recall cards on 7-, 14- or 28-day half-lives | No outcome data found [E]. Matuschak: highlight lists lose their context [experience] |
| Spaced prompts | Quantum Country | Designed questions on growing intervals | [data], for readers who keep going |

**Reading** [L]. Schedules work when the content is built for recall (prompts), or when a person reviews their own fresh fragments. For a person's ideas, practitioners revisit when present work calls for them.

## 9. The convergent view

### Where they agree
1. **A small entry layer gives the big picture.** Index, entry points, structure notes, MOCs, a garden's many entries: short lists of starting points, made and extended by the person as the collection grows [experience; Luhmann's few entries per keyword are archive data, note 02].
2. **Structure grows and gets rebuilt.** No fixed taxonomy up front; Luhmann rebuilt his index four times [experience + archive data].
3. **You revisit for a present purpose.** Ahrens's context question, Matuschak's interest-led upkeep, Forte's summaries made on the way through, Johnson's paragraph used as a query [experience].
4. **The concrete beats the abstract.** Show the thing, and keep its context [experience + argument].
5. **Add, don't overwrite.** Lee's appended entries, Luhmann's kept cards, Ango's journals [experience].
6. **Graphs serve upkeep, not understanding** [experience].

### Where they disagree
1. **Tree or network.** Scheper wants a tree. Fast adds hierarchy in structure notes. Milo's MOCs overlap freely. Matuschak prefers association but asks for outline notes. Engelbart and Brander dissolve the split: a hierarchy is one view over a network [argument].
2. **Who makes the overview.** The person (Luhmann, Milo, Ango), or a machine that proposes (Johnson, Lee, Karpathy) [experience on both sides].
3. **Calendar or need.** Readwise and Ango's reviews run on a calendar; Matuschak's revisiting follows interest [experience].
4. **Time or topic.** Lee makes time first-class; Appleton puts topography over timelines [experience; argument].
5. **Keep everything, or select.** Bush and Lee keep it all. Kidd, Sellen & Whittaker and Westenberg (note 02) argue for selection [mixed].

### What rests on data, and what on anecdote
- **Data:** Quantum Country's retention (memory prompts, self-selected readers); Kidd's 12 knowledge workers; Masicampo & Baumeister's plan-making experiments; the Obsidian case study, Luhmann's archive counts and Whittaker's email study (all in note 02). **None tests a form for revisiting one's own ideas** [E].
- **Experience:** hubs, structure notes, MOCs, PARA, progressive summarization, the Hemingway bridge, graph views for upkeep, reviews of reviews, random revisits, Monocle, DEVONthink [E].
- **Argument:** Bush's trails, Engelbart's views, Matuschak's case against hierarchy, Appleton's topography, Victor's "Magic Ink", Brander's links, Heylighen & Vidal [E].

## 10. The sketch round, read through this lens [L]

- **Sketch A** (topic outline with conflict pairs) had a hub's shape but not a hub's origins. Practitioners' hubs hold a few entry points, in the owner's words, made when needed. A's 22 ideas in AI-chosen words were closer to a full table of contents written by someone else. The user's note, "This is what I think right?" (GOAL §1), fits Kidd's point: the marks are on the person, and a summary in someone else's words may not trigger recognition.
- **Sketch B** (stable map) gave the big-picture shape the user leans towards: "Leaning towards B but still not very helpful." Seeing the shape of a body of work is the one use graph defenders claim. B also inherits the critics' point: a shape shows that links exist, not what they mean.
- **Neither** offered a trail or an antecedent view; "ask about this" sat outside both. Yet both of the user's "ask" tries were questions about their past: the need practitioners serve by asking and following trails.
- **A caveat on transfer.** Practitioner forms assume a person who writes notes. This user said "I dont really have too many notes" (GOAL §1); their material is chats and work. The forms that transfer best are those whose owners did not hand-curate everything: Johnson's associations over a chosen archive, Lee's search over everything he wrote, and Bush's trails, made while working.

- Not opened: Ahrens's book, *Building a Second Brain*, Newton's article, Sellen & Whittaker's full text, the Obsidian case study's full text (the PDF could not be read here), Johnson's original in the *Times*, and the Zettelkasten forum threads (search summaries only).

## Implications for the overview

Forms ranked for each need. The rankings are [L]; the grades say what supports each form.

| Need | Rank | Form | Who supports it | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **(1) Big picture** | 1 | **A hub**: per topic, a few of the person's ideas as one-line claims in their own words, each with one work cue, conflicts as pairs, everything else one step away | Luhmann, Ahrens, Fast, Milo, Appleton; Capstone for the work cue | [experience]; few entries per keyword [data, archive] |
| | 2 | **A "what I came to think" digest**: layered by week, month and year, built from the same items | Ango; Forte and Allen | [experience] |
| | 3 | **A map as a view of shape**: clusters, isolated ideas, conflict lines; or a local graph one or two steps around one idea | Konik; Ango; Engelbart's network view | [experience], for seeing shape only |
| **(2) Get back into work** | 1 | **A bridge** for each piece of work: where it stands, the next step, open questions, and the work itself | Hemingway via Forte; Engelbart's Joe; Ahrens | [experience]; plan-making [data, indirect] |
| | 2 | **One piece of work in one place**: its ideas, decisions and artifacts together | PARA; intermediate packets | [experience] |
| | 3 | **A time-ordered trail** of that work | Lee; Ango | [experience] |
| **(3) Question about my past** | 1 | **Ask in the chat**, with the answer returned as a trail: the person's items, work and sources it drew on, in order | Bush, Luhmann, Johnson, Lee; the user's two "ask" tries | [experience] + [argument]; the user's own use (one person) |
| | 2 | **"Why do I think this?"**: an idea's antecedents, the conversations and work behind it | Engelbart | [argument] |
| | 3 | **Search** over one's own record | Lee; Johnson | [experience]. Note 25: people still prefer navigating to their own files [data] |

**An AI-built topic outline.** Its shape has the most support: practitioners' hubs, structure notes and MOCs are outlines [experience]. But every practitioner's hub is made by its owner, starts small and grows when a region gets crowded. Ango and Kidd locate the understanding in that making, and in the person [E]. An AI-written outline risks reading "like an answer", as four text formats did (GOAL §2, 2026-10-14) [L]. If it is used [L]:
- keep it to entry points, a few per topic;
- use the person's own words verbatim;
- let them rename, reorder and pin, which makes the making cheap;
- treat each line as a door to a trail or to the work, not as content.

**Grade: [experience] for the form; [argument] at best for AI authorship; no data.**

**An AI-built map or graph.** Practitioners support graphs only for upkeep and for seeing the shape of a body of work. None uses one to navigate or to understand, and no study supports either use [experience]. Matuschak warns that a display that never changes stops being seen, while note 25 found that a stable layout helps orientation [E]. Best use: a second view for clusters, isolated ideas and conflicts, or a shallow local graph around the idea in hand. It should not be the front door [L]. **Grade: [experience], for shape and upkeep only.**

**Hub or index views.** This is the strongest agreement in this slice: Luhmann's register, Ahrens's entry points, Fast's structure notes, Milo's MOCs, Appleton's many entries and Matuschak's outline notes [experience; archive data on few entries per keyword]. The hub is the best-supported first view [L]. How it differs from sketch A: fewer lines, the person's own words, a work cue on each, and a path onward from every line (ask, trail, antecedents). Putting the map second reorders the user's 2026-10-15 pick ("Map"); that is a question for the user, not a decision [L].

**Revisit or expand.** Practitioners rarely separate the two. They revisit because a present problem calls for it, and the box "answers" with associations (Luhmann, Johnson, Matuschak) [experience]. But the new thinking is the person's, done in conversation with the material, not written into the store [experience]. This supports keeping the overview revisit-only, with "apply to my problem" one step away in the chat, as note 25 proposed. It does not settle the user's open question [L].

**What to measure next** [L]. Whether the person recognizes their own ideas from one-line claims (the "not what I think" flags); time to the first useful cue for each need; and how often they ask and follow a trail rather than browse.
